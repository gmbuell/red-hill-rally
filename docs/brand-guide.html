The Big Idea

Launch-day energy, hometown heart

Red Hill's visual voice blends two feelings that always appear together: the upward launch energy of the Rockets — bold caps, stars, motion lines, a rocket mid-flight — and hometown warmth — handwritten script, hearts, thank-yous, and a palette pulled from launch morning: red and gold against a clear blue sky. The warmth is the feeling; the standard is discipline. Every piece should carry the heart of a school community and the craft of a real brand: friendly, educational, and inspiring — spirited, never sloppy, and never corporate.

01 · Color

The Palette

Six colors, always in the same roles. Navy leads, red punctuates, gold celebrates, ice paper grounds, sky organizes, slate supports.

Red Hill Navy
#0A2B4E

The lead voice. All primary text, structural elements, icons, buttons, and the rocket itself. When in doubt, it's navy.

Rocket Red
#B92025

The punctuation. One red moment per composition — see The Red Rule below the grid. Deep enough to carry white text.

Sunlight Gold
#F5B70F

Celebration only: stars, rocket flames, suns, and script over navy. Never text on light backgrounds — it can't hold contrast there.

Ice Paper
#F7FAFD

The paper. A cool, sky-biased near-white — the ground for every piece, print and digital. True #FFFFFF is reserved for knockout text and shapes on navy and red.

Sky Wash
#E9F1F9

Quiet panels behind icon rows and info blocks, hero grounds, and the sky in illustrated scenes. Organizes without competing.

Slate
#5A6474

Utility only: captions, secondary labels, and hairline rules. Passes AA for small text on every light ground — never for headlines or brand moments.

The Red Rule: red gets one moment per composition — one word you'd stress aloud, or one short element worn all-red.

The fine print
  • Stress is a single word you'd stress saying the line aloud — always inside a multi-word line, never a word alone on its line.
  • Voice is one whole short element worn all-red: a label, a kicker, a script phrase.
  • A matched set of repeated labels reads as one element — repetition is rhythm.
  • Never body copy; never headlines by default.
  • Beyond text, red is ribbon banners, brush bands, hearts, fins, and — in grid builds — one structural mass a knockout mark can sit in, whether a block or an edge rail.
  • Budget red by area: a structural red spend usually exhausts the allowance on its own, and when it does, text and labels stay navy.

One color lives outside the palette: Leaf Green #7FB069, for grass, bushes, and stems in illustrated scenes only — never in text, icons, or layout. Everything else, including scene skies and clouds, comes from the six above.

Rough proportions on any layout: ice paper dominates, navy carries the message, sky organizes, red punctuates, gold accents sparingly.

02 · Typography

Three Voices, One Choir

Display · Oswald SemiBold
Proceeds go back to Red Hill!

The megaphone. Tall, tightly-set caps for headlines, banners, and calls to action. Always uppercase, reserved for headline scale — never body sizes. Navy or red on light grounds; white when knocked out of navy or red. On hero headlines and ribbon banners, set the caps on a slight upward arc (see below) for launch energy; leave arcs off body-adjacent headings.

Arc Treatment

A gentle rise — the middle of the line sits roughly one cap-height above the ends. Reserve it for one heading per composition, usually the topmost. Never arc script or body text, and never bend text into a full circle.

Script · Kaushan Script
Thank you for making a difference

The handwritten heart. Reserved for greetings, titles, and emotional hooks — thank you, any amount, together, special thanks.

One script moment per composition, for feelings — never information.

The fine print
  • A lockup's adjacent script-caps-script alternation counts as one moment; a script line separated from it by other elements is a second.
  • Never uppercase, never information — dates, prices, instructions — and never smaller than 18px.
  • Gratitude doesn't require script: body text can say thank you.
  • Over navy, script may be gold or white.
Body & UI · Nunito Sans
Once you're signed in, you can view the school calendar, join the PTA, sign up to volunteer, and more. Bold for emphasis, and SPACED CAPS FOR LABELS.

The workhorse. All paragraphs, buttons, captions, and fine print. Rounded and warm, so even the fine print feels friendly. Labels get 800 weight, uppercase, and letter-spacing — slate for wayfinding (section numbers, captions, running labels), navy otherwise. A kicker introducing a headline may spend the composition's red moment (The Red Rule, Section 01) — as "The Big Idea" does above. Body text stays 16px or larger. One technical exception: hex values and code may render in the system monospace.

Sub-headings · Nunito Sans ExtraBold
Create your account today, simplify your school year.

The body voice, speaking up. The same Nunito Sans as the paragraphs, pushed to ExtraBold at generous size for prominent sub-headlines and introductory sentences — anywhere the display caps would shout but body text would whisper. Always navy, always sentence case. Not a fourth face: sub-headings earn their weight from size and boldness, so the whole page below headline level stays one warm family.

The Wordmark
Red Hill

"Red Hill" set in Oswald SemiBold caps with slight letter-spacing — navy on light grounds, white on navy — optionally on the gentle arc. What makes it the wordmark rather than a headline is discipline: nothing else shares its line, and clear space equal to the cap height of the R holds on all sides. Minimum size 24px. As a logotype it's exempt from contrast requirements, but don't lean on that: set it on approved grounds anyway. This guide's own cover leads with it, "Brand Guide" stepped down beneath. A script "Red Hill" is a greeting flourish, never the wordmark.

Every
Donation Counts!
…and any amount helps.

The signature lockup: script → caps → script. Alternate the voices line by line. Red is written stress — this lockup could stress "Every" or "Counts!", and stresses "Counts!" because a stressed word must sit inside a multi-word line. When no word takes the stress, the line stays navy, as on this guide's own cover.

03 · Motifs

The Spirit Kit

All visuals are flat vector with solid fills and solid shapes, with one signature exception: the red outlined heart. (The Sticker Cut's die-cut edge is a border around a solid motif; it has its own card below.) And every piece carries at least one Spirit Kit motif, however austere the layout: without one, even a perfectly on-palette piece stops being Red Hill. When a piece needs to feel hand-made, reach for irregular contours — a brush edge, a wavy grass line, a hand-hung tilt — and keep surfaces clean and flat.

The Badge Rocket

The primary mark. Solid navy silhouette, white porthole, three red speed dashes — the version that behaves like a logo, built for badges, seals, and the heart lockup. On navy or red grounds it runs as an all-white knockout, porthole in the ground color, dashes white or omitted — or it keeps its colors inside a sticker cut.

The Red Rocket

The celebration rocket. Red body, navy fins and porthole ring, and a full, curving gold plume with a red inner tongue — the flame is drawn with a curve. The big expressive version for banners and spirit pieces, always with a dotted trail's worth of room to fly.

The Dart

The accent. A small, simplified dart — navy body, red fins, streak exhaust — for corners, dividers, and above headlines, where it stays legible at 24px. Every rocket flies mid-flight at a rising angle.

The Sticker Cut

Any motif may wear a solid, even border — a sticker slapped on the page. The cut runs between the parts too, so fins sit apart from the body. White is the classic cut, and it's what lets the rocket — navy fins and all — land on a navy band. On light grounds the cut may instead match the ground color: an invisible edge whose whole job is those crisp gaps. The motif inside stays flat and solid — this is an edge, not line-art.

Star Scatter

Confetti. Two or three stars in mixed sizes and colors clustered near a headline or motif — asymmetric, like they just burst. Always star-shaped.

Hearts

Always Rocket Red, never navy — and always on light grounds: red can't hold contrast against navy (2.2:1), so hearts never sit on navy or red. Solid hearts work as icons and symbols of care; a small outlined heart tucks at the end of a thank-you like a signature.

Ribbon Banner

Frames a key sentence in white knockout caps that follow the ribbon's arch. On navy fills, a leading warm word may be gold script; on red fills every word stays white — gold on red is 3.5:1, too low for script sizes. Swallow-tail ends step slightly below the band, a degree or two of tilt — hand-hung, never perfectly straight. Red or navy fills only.

Brush Stroke

A painted swipe of navy or red behind script or under a word — tapered ends, a dry-brush streak or two, a stray fleck. Adds energy and a hand-made edge in one move. Its full-width sibling, the Brush Band, carries a whole line.

The Brush Band

The ribbon's energetic sibling: one full-width stroke carrying one short line of white knockout caps. Ribbon frames celebration; band frames action — sign-ups, launches, calls to action. Band text sets straight (the arch belongs to the ribbon), fills are navy or red — a red band spends the red allowance — and white bands live only on non-ice grounds where the edge can read. One band per piece, and a piece with a band skips the swipe.

Sparks & Trails

Sunburst dashes flank a headline like a cheer; a dotted trail follows the rocket — round dots on a loose, curving path, tracing the flight it just took. Both say "something exciting is happening here."

Environmental Vectors

The schoolhouse is the anchor scene — gabled navy roof, arched red door, flag flying — supported by flat clouds, bushes, grass, and flowers. Grass and foliage use Leaf Green; skies and clouds use Sky Wash; suns are gold. Characters and icons stay in the core palette.

Program Icons

Simplified icons for programs: an open book with a red star for enrichment, a sneaker for PE and recess, a palette for the arts. Navy base plus one or two accent colors; one object, no fine detail — legible at 40px.

Navy ribbon banners can mix voices: gold script for the warm phrase, white caps for the message. Red ribbons keep every word white.

The brush band frames action the way the ribbon frames celebration: straight white caps, no arch, navy or red fill, dry-brush streaks and a stray fleck at the ends. It sits square — the ribbon above keeps this view's tilt, and two tilts never share a view.

04 · Layout & Application

How Pieces Come Together

One paper for everything

Every piece — flyers, posters, web pages, forms — sits on Ice Paper #F7FAFD, a cool, sky-biased near-white. One ground means print and digital always feel like the same brand, and red and gold read hotter against it.

Sky wash organizes

Section bands, icon rows, and info panels sit in Sky Wash — the same blue as the illustrated sky, so panels feel like air. Warmth comes from gold, script, and the voice; the paper stays cool.

Composition. Every piece commits to one spine. The default is the spirit stack: a centered vertical build — motif, then lockup, then ribbon or panel, then supporting detail — that balances itself and always feels like a poster. The alternative is the grid build: everything hangs flush-left on a strict grid, oversized type and generous whitespace, with one bold mass counterweighting the type. Choose the stack when the feeling leads, the grid when the information leads — and never mix spines in one piece. In both builds, body text stays left-aligned inside its column: centering is for the spine, never the sentences.

The grid build in practice. Open with a hairline masthead — organization flush-left, date flush-right — anchored to the content edge: when a rail claims an edge of the sheet, the grid's origin moves with it. The counterweight to the type mass is one bold element, any of three forms: a navy or red block a knockout mark sits in, a single large motif, or a structural rail or band bled off the edge — and the band may itself be an illustrated scene, a grass strip serving as both mass and motif. Script keeps its warmth here, flush-left on its own line. And motifs may inhabit the structure instead of decorating it: a rocket climbing a rail, its trail running inside the band.

Panels and rows. Lists of programs or benefits sit in a strip: icon above label, items separated by thin vertical rules, three to five items — enough to show breadth, few enough to read at a car-line glance. The strip has two grounds. Sky Wash is the default — quiet organization inside a busy page, labels in bold navy caps. Navy is the announcement variant, for when the row itself is the star: gold caps labels, white body text, white hairline rules, and icons as solid white knockouts — never line drawings. Gold is a label color on navy only; on light grounds labels stay navy or slate.

Edges are horizons. The boundary between two grounds is a stage. A panel's top edge can be the ground line the schoolhouse sits on; a navy band's rim, the surface a rocket launches from; a hairline, the path the dart flies above. One figure on one edge is plenty — it's the move that makes a piece look composed instead of assembled.

Small against big. Scale drama runs both ways. Huge type is one kind of energy; a small motif alone in a generous field is the other — a tiny rocket high in an empty ice-paper sky reads as flight and wonder. When a motif shrinks, let the whitespace around it do the work.

Photography. Event photos join a layout as blocks: sharp-edged rectangles set flush on the grid and treated as one bold mass — a photo can carry a grid build as its counterweight. Text sits beside a photo, corners stay square, and the block sits on the grid. In a spirit stack, a photo may take the composition's one tilt, like a snapshot taped to the fridge; in a grid build it stays square like everything else.

Tilt with intent. One tilt per view — a view being what the eye takes in at once. A ribbon, a badge, a brush band, a taped-down photo may rotate a degree or two; it's the hand-made wink. A poster is one view, so one tilt. A tall stacked piece is several views, so a tilted badge in its hero and a tilted ribbon at its close can coexist — but two tilts never share a view, and type blocks, panels, and anything on the grid never tilt. If everything tilts, nothing does. To know whether any device belongs in a grid build, ask where its energy lives: in its contour, or in its placement. Contour energy — script curves, brush edges, a wavy grass line — travels anywhere, because the grid governs placement and doesn't care about edges. Placement energy — tilt, arc, the ribbon's hand-hung hang — stays in the stack, because the grid is a placement discipline and those devices exist to defy it.

The device budget: a typical piece picks two or three devices and lets the rest sit out.

The fine print
  • The one-of-each rules — arc, tilt (per view), brush stroke, star scatter, sparks, scene — are ceilings.
  • Reach for everything at once only when the occasion is genuinely maximal.
  • Even then, ask which device the piece would miss least — and cut it.

Buttons. Buttons are solid Red Hill Navy, pill-shaped, with white 700-weight text and a directional arrow; hover and pressed states fill Rocket Red. One primary button per view; secondary actions get the outlined variant. The pill shape is deliberate contrast: everything printed is sharp-cornered and flat, so anything rounded is something you can press — never use pill shapes on badges, panels, or anything else that isn't interactive. Keyboard focus gets a visible offset ring: navy on light grounds, gold on navy grounds — each side of the ring holds at least 3:1 against what it touches.

05 · Voice

How Red Hill Sounds

Gratitude first, community always. Red Hill talks like a thankful neighbor.

Lead with thanks

  • Open every ask — donations, volunteering, membership — with appreciation: "Thank you for investing in our students, staff & school."
  • Name and celebrate helpers: "Special thanks to the Talbert Family!"
  • Announcements may lead with the news instead — but the community heart arrives before the call to action: "100% of proceeds support our students."

Always "our," never "the"

  • It's our students, our school, our community — the reader is already part of it.
  • Invitations: "Create your account today, simplify your school year."

Every bit counts

  • Remove pressure from every ask: "Any amount is appreciated and makes an impact!"
  • Excitement, never scarcity — no "before we sell out," no countdown clocks.
  • Exclamation points are welcome — one per sentence, earned by genuine enthusiasm.
06 · Accessibility

Everyone Reads the Poster

Every digital piece — web pages, forms, emails — meets WCAG 2.2 AA. Print doesn't answer to WCAG, but it answers to the same eyes: posters and flyers use the same approved color pairs and size minimums, so the standard travels with the brand. The palette was built so this happens by default — follow the roles above and you're compliant. The specifics (SC numbers refer to WCAG 2.2):

Text contrast (SC 1.4.3). Body-size text uses only combinations rated 4.5:1 or better in the table in Section 01. Large text — 24px+ regular or 19px+ bold, which covers all Oswald headlines and ExtraBold sub-headings — needs 3:1, and every approved pair clears that with room to spare. Script below 24px follows the 4.5:1 body rule like everything else (every approved pair already does). Two exemptions apply: the "Red Hill" wordmark and rocket logo lockup are logotypes with no contrast requirement, and text inside busy illustrated scenes is treated as part of the picture — but don't put information there in the first place.

Non-text contrast (SC 1.4.11). Anything a reader must identify to use or understand — button shapes, form fields, focus rings, program icons, chart segments — holds 3:1 against what it touches. In practice: navy components on light grounds (13:1+), navy hairlines separating adjacent light fills (as in the proportion bar above), and the two-ring focus rule from Section 04. Purely decorative elements — star scatter, sparks and trails, Sky Wash panels — are exempt and stay exempt only as long as they carry no information.

Text spacing. Body leading is at least 1.5 (this page uses 1.6), and the gap between paragraphs is at least half a line beyond normal leading, so paragraph breaks read as breaks — baseline-to-baseline across a paragraph boundary comes to roughly 1.6× a normal line advance. Never set body copy solid.

Minimum sizes. Body text 16px+, captions and labels 13px+ in slate, navy, or red, script 18px+, icons legible at 40px. Gold text exists only on navy, at any size.

07 · Guardrails

Keep It Red Hill

Do
  • Alternate script and caps line-by-line in lockups
  • Make red one text moment: one earned word, or one whole short element
  • Arc one caps heading per stack composition, gently
  • Keep the rocket flying upward at an angle
  • Ground every piece on ice paper; let sky wash panels organize
  • Check new color pairs against the contrast table before use
  • Scatter a few stars near headlines, asymmetrically
  • Carry at least one Spirit Kit motif, even on the sparest piece
  • Frame a call to action in a brush band — straight white caps on navy or red
  • Sticker-cut a motif to land it on a same-color ground
  • Say thank you before asking — or lead an announcement with the news, never with pressure
Don't
  • Set script type in all caps or use it for dates and prices
  • Put red on a word you wouldn't stress aloud, a word alone on its line, or a full headline
  • Put gold text on ice paper or sky — it fails contrast (1.7:1)
  • Color hearts navy, use them outside red, or set them on navy or red grounds
  • Stack more than one script moment per composition
  • Use gradients, drop shadows, or outlined illustration (the red outlined heart and the sticker cut's edge are the exceptions)
  • Add grain, aging, or distressing — hand-made is contour, never texture
  • Arc more than one heading, or bend script text at all
  • Let the tone go corporate — no "utilize," no "per our policy"
Thank you!
